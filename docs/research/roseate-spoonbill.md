## The Roseate Spoonbill as a symbol
- **Aztec / Nahua (central Mexico).** The spoonbill is tlauhquechol, also teoquechol. Molina's 1571 dictionary glosses the word as 'pluma rica y bermeja', rich vermilion plumage, so the bird's name doubled as the word for its precious red feathers. Karttunen (1992) says the quechol element refers not to the color but apparently to the bird's characteristic sweeping motion of the neck. In Florentine Codex Book 3 the red spoonbill is listed among the birds of Tollan 'which spoke very well'. Torquemada called it a water bird with valuable feathers. A sweep that is also a name, and a color that is also wealth: the strongest bridge on this page. [s11] [s12]
- **Tupi (Brazil).** The species name ajaja comes from the Tupi name for the bird, recorded by Georg Marcgrave as 'Aiaia' in 1648 (Historia Naturalis Brasiliae); Linnaeus based his 1758 description largely on Marcgrave. Naming fact only; no Tupi meaning or story was found. Use as a one-line credit, not a hook. [s5]
- **Chinese naming.** Spoonbills are 琵鷺 pílù in Chinese because the bill resembles the pipa (琵琶 pípá), the pear-shaped lute; 鷺 lù is the egret and heron word. This species is 粉紅琵鷺 fěnhóng pílù, the pink spoonbill. A Libra-Venus detail that is real: the bird is named for a musical instrument. The CGTN source is about the black-faced spoonbill (Macao's tourism mascot Mak Mak), so frame the pipa origin as true of the whole genus name, not as roseate-specific lore. [s13] [s14] [s15]
- **United States conservation history.** Spoonbills were virtually eliminated from the United States in the 1800s, largely as a side effect of plume hunters destroying the wader colonies they shared with egrets, though their own pink feathers were also sold for hats. About two dozen breeding pairs remained on the whole Gulf Coast around 1920; Texas had 179 birds that year. Robert Porter Allen of Audubon called the bill an instrument that 'must serve as both eyes and hands'. The bird is now a recovery story (Breeding Bird Survey: about 6.5% a year growth 1966 to 2019). [s7] [s8] [s9] [s6]
- **Unsourced, do not use.** 'Spoonbill spirit animal = grace, abundance, balance' claims on totem blogs; no Indigenous Florida (Seminole, Calusa) or Caribbean story about the spoonbill could be found and opened. Do not invent one.

The spoonbill fits both: it walks into murky water and sweeps it evenly, taking what the shallows offer, and its color is literally what it has been fed. A Libra whose balance is kept by the company and the food around it.

- **Conflict.** Our reading: sweeps around it. Avoids the open fight (Libra), assumes good faith (Pig). When it does speak, it speaks fairly and once. Practice: name the imbalance while the color is still there.

## Fact audit of the live page
- **IMPRECISE (wrong bird in common use)**: Header hanzi 鹮 (rail__han and serial-han).
  鹮 huán (traditional 䴉) is the family word for ibises and spoonbills (Threskiornithidae) and in everyday Chinese names the ibis, e.g. 朱鹮 zhūhuán, the crested ibis. A spoonbill is 琵鷺 pílù; this species is 粉紅琵鷺 fěnhóng pílù. Recommend 琵鷺 pílù (or 粉紅琵鷺 fěnhóng pílù) in traditional form, with pinyin. [s16] [s14] [s15]
- **OK (first half) / UNVERIFIABLE (second half)**: Rose-pink plumage comes from carotenoid pigments in the crustaceans it eats; birds on a poor diet grow paler, and the color fades without the right food.
  Diet-derived carotenoid (canthaxanthin, plus astaxanthin) is confirmed; SF Zoo names the crustacean diet. Audubon and NWF say the diet is mostly small fish (up to 85%), so 'crustaceans' alone is narrow. No source opened says poor-diet birds grow paler; Wikipedia and SF Zoo attribute shade to age, breeding state and location. Cut 'poor diet grow paler' or source it. [s5] [s10] [s9] [s8]
- **OK (soften)**: Spoon-shaped bill swept side to side through shallow water to filter prey by touch rather than sight.
  Side-to-side sweep confirmed. All About Birds says 'feeling and looking'; Audubon says 'detecting prey by feel'. 'Filter' is loose: it snaps prey shut on contact, it does not filter like a flamingo. Suggest 'catches prey by touch, snapping the bill shut when something brushes it'. [s6] [s9]
- **OK**: Detects prey through touch-sensitive receptors in the bill, feeding in muddy water where nothing can be seen.
  Houston Audubon: nerve endings along the bill sense vibrations and trigger the snap; NWF: reflex when contact stimulates nerves at the tip. Birds of the World (via Houston Audubon) says the mechanics of tactilocation are not well understood, so keep the claim simple. [s7] [s8]
- **OK**: Typically nest and forage in colonies, often alongside herons, ibises and other wading birds.
  All About Birds: nests in colonies with egrets, ibises and herons. Wikipedia adds egrets follow spoonbills to catch disturbed prey (beater-follower). [s6] [s5]
- **OK (first half) / UNVERIFIABLE (second half)**: Adults develop a bald, greenish head as they mature, a trait shared with several long-lived wading birds and unusual among more heavily feathered relatives.
  Bare greenish head in adults, feathered white head in immatures: confirmed. 'Shared with several long-lived wading birds and unusual among relatives' has no source; cut it. [s5] [s7] [s10]
- **OK (nuance)**: Populations in parts of the southeastern US were hunted nearly to local extinction for their feathers in the late 1800s and have since recovered under protection.
  Supported (Houston Audubon, SF Zoo, Wikipedia). Nuance: Audubon/Kaufman calls the US loss largely a side effect of plume hunters destroying wader colonies; NWF says egret plume hunting made spoonbills abandon shared colonies. Around 1920 about two dozen pairs remained on the Gulf Coast. Florida Bay numbers fell again after a 1972 peak, so 'recovered' is true range-wide, less so in Florida Bay. [s7] [s8] [s9] [s5]
- **OK**: Purple Star (Zi Wei Dou Shu) claims.
  No 'Purple Star already suspects' star assignment on this page; the Purple Star block correctly says a chart is cast from lunar birth date and hour.
