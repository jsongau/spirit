## The Loggerhead Shrike as a symbol
- **Nahua (Aztec), Florentine Codex.** The Nahuatl name tetzompah (tetzonpan; also tetzompan mamana) is glossed as the Loggerhead Shrike. Sahagún's Florentine Codex, Book 11, describes a bill pointed like a metal awl and a bird that impales its catch, mice and lizards, on trees and on maguey leaves; the codex also paints it (fol. 49r). Ethnobiologist E. S. Hunn: 'The behavior leaves no doubt.' A 16th-century Indigenous record of this exact species. [s6] [s7]
- **Chinese, classical (Shijing and Liji).** 鵙 jú is an old name for the shrike (鵙，伯勞也). Shijing, Odes of Bin, 七月: 七月鳴鵙，八月載績 (qī yuè míng jú, bā yuè zài jì), the shrike calls in the seventh month and in the eighth the spinning begins. Liji 月令: 仲夏之月，鵙始鳴 (zhòng xià zhī yuè, jú shǐ míng), in midsummer the shrike first calls. The shrike as a calendar bird: its voice marks the turn to work. [s8]
- **Chinese idiom (Yuefu folk song).** 勞燕分飛 láo yàn fēn fēi, the shrike and the swallow fly apart, from the folk song 東飛伯勞歌 (dōng fēi bóláo gē), where the shrike flies east and the swallow west. Used for lovers or couples who part. 勞 here is 伯勞 bóláo, shrike. [s9] [s10]
- **Latin and English naming.** Lanius is Latin for butcher; Lanius ludovicianus reads roughly 'Louis' butcher' (type specimen from Louisiana). 'Loggerhead' is akin to blockhead and refers to the big head. In the American South it is called the butcher bird. 'Shrike' comes from Old English scric, a shriek. [s11] [s12] [s13]
- **English, Elizabethan.** George Turberville (1575) wrote that the shrike will stand at perch on a tree or post and make an exceedingly lamentable cry, as a lure. The patient perch and the deceiving voice were noticed 450 years ago. (Turberville likely meant a European shrike, not the Loggerhead; use as shrike-family lore only.) [s11]
- **Unsourced, do not use.** Generic 'shrike spirit animal / totem' meanings and any Native American shrike story for this species: none found in an ethnographic source. Japanese hayanie (shrike larder lore) belongs to the Bull-headed Shrike, a different species; not researched, do not transfer. 

## The fusion (our reading)
- **Temperament.** Our reading: double Fire, but two different fires. Aries is cardinal Fire, the spark that starts; the Snake is yin Fire, the banked coal. The shrike holds both: a songbird body that sits on one post for a long time, then drops once. Patience used as a weapon, not as a mood.
- **Gifts.** Our reading: reads open ground well; acts once and cleanly; stores resources for the lean season; improvises a grip it was never given (no talons, so it uses the thorn). The Snake's sharp mind keeps the Aries charge from being wasted.
- **Shadow.** Our reading: the larder of grievances. The Snake's long memory plus the Aries temper that the Almanac calls short becomes a quiet ledger: hurts filed, never voiced, and a strike that arrives without a warning anyone heard. Also the Snake's 'lack of scruples' note: the end can start to excuse the method.
- **Love.** Our reading: Mars-ruled and yin. Courts by showing what it has built rather than saying what it feels (the provisioned larder as proof, per the Yosef and Pinshow title). Loyal and watchful; shrinks from partners who need constant talk. 勞燕分飛 is its old fear: two birds that fly apart at the fork without a word.
- **Money.** Our reading: a stocker. Keeps reserves on the thorn for bad weather, and is good under tight deadlines (Snake). Risk: hoarding past usefulness, or one big decisive bet made from a long watch that was not as complete as it felt.
- **Conflict.** Our reading: goes still first, strikes second, explains never. The fix is to speak while it is still small, before it goes on the thorn.

## Fact audit of the live page
- **OK**: Header hanzi 鵙 (rail__han and serial-han), shown with pinyin jú.
  鵙 jú is a genuine classical name for the shrike (Longkan Shoujian: 鵙，伯勞也; Shijing 七月鳴鵙). It names shrikes in general, not this species. Modern everyday word is 伯勞 bóláo (traditional), 伯劳 (simplified). I found no opened source for a species name for Lanius ludovicianus in Chinese, so do not add one. Pinyin jú is correct. [s8] [s10]
- **OK (soften)**: Hooked black bill carries a pair of small pointed notches, tomial teeth, used to strike the back of a prey animal's neck and sever the spinal cord.
  Audubon: targeted attack at the back of the neck, a hooked barb slips between vertebrae and cuts the spinal cord. Sustaita et al. 2018 say the tomial projections are 'thought to' penetrate between cervical vertebrae and cause partial paralysis, and add that violent head-roll shaking may itself damage the neck. Suggest: 'thought to slip between the neck vertebrae; shrikes also kill by shaking prey hard'. One tomial tooth on each side of the upper bill, so 'a pair' is fine. [s13] [s14] [s12]
- **OK**: Impales insects, lizards, small rodents and occasionally other birds on thorns, spines or barbed wire, building a larder to store food, tear apart large prey and let toxic prey sit until the poison breaks down.
  Prey list matches ADW (mostly mice, plus insects, small amphibians, small birds) and the Nahuatl codex (mice, lizards). Storage and dismembering per Wikipedia Shrike. Toxic aging is documented for lubber grasshoppers (Yosef and Whitman 1992). [s17] [s11] [s15] [s6]
- **UNVERIFIABLE (details)**: A well-stocked larder has been linked to greater breeding success; in the closely related Southern Grey Shrike, females paired with better-provisioned males laid more eggs and raised more young.
  The paper's title (Yosef and Pinshow 1989, The Auk) confirms cache size influences female mate choice and reproductive success. I could not open the full text, so the species name and the 'laid more eggs and raised more young' wording are unconfirmed. The study was on a Negev grey shrike population whose species label has shifted between Great Grey/Northern (L. excubitor) and Southern Grey (L. meridionalis); calling it 'Southern Grey Shrike' as settled fact is risky. Safe line: 'In a desert population of grey shrikes, males with bigger caches attracted mates sooner and bred more successfully (Yosef and Pinshow 1989)' after a human checks the PDF. [s16] [s11]
- **OK**: A true passerine with small gripping songbird feet, no raptor talons; lacking strength to pin prey it wedges the kill on a thorn or barb.
  ADW: 'due to their lack of talons or claws they must impale their prey.' [s17]
- **OK (soften 'single')**: Hunts by day from a single exposed perch (fence post, wire, bare branch) in open grassland or scrub, scanning the ground and dropping in a short direct flight.
  Wikipedia: sit-and-wait hunters that stalk prey by hawking and diving from elevated perches, preferred perches about 4 m high. It uses several perches, so 'a single' is overstated; say 'from an exposed perch'. [s12]
- **WRONG**: Observed impaling toxic prey including monarch butterflies and toads, leaving them on the thorn for up to several days until the toxins break down.
  The documented case is the eastern lubber grasshopper (Romalea guttata) in Florida: shrikes could eat lubbers after they had aged 1 to 2 days (Yosef and Whitman 1992, Evolutionary Ecology). I found no study of Loggerhead Shrikes aging monarchs or toads. Correct to: 'impales toxic lubber grasshoppers and waits a day or two before eating them'. [s15] [s11] [s13]
- **WRONG**: Purple Star section: 'the Aries Snake crossing draws Seven Killings (七殺 qīshā) more often than chance would allow' and gives it Wu Qu (武曲 wǔqū).
  Zi Wei Dou Shu stars are placed from the lunar birth date and hour; the Western sun sign plays no part. There is no data showing any crossing draws a star 'more often than chance'. The page does add a disclaimer that this is its own reading, but the frequency claim contradicts it. Cut the frequency sentence at minimum. [s18]
- **OK**: Hero copy: 'A small gray-and-white songbird of North America's open country'.
  Wikipedia: medium-sized passerine, grey above, white to pale grey below, black mask. ADW gives 8 to 10 inches, about 46 g. 'Small' is fine colloquially. Note for the rewrite: it is in decline since the 1960s and critically endangered in Canada (fewer than 35 known breeding pairs). [s12] [s17]
