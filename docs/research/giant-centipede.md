## The Giant Centipede as a symbol
- **Chinese: the Five Poisons 五毒 wǔdú.** The centipede 蜈蚣 wúgōng is one of the Five Poisons (with snake, scorpion, toad, and lizard or spider), shown on charms and clothing around the Duanwu (Dragon Boat) Festival, the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, to turn harm away. A Ming silk panel at The Met shows the set. [c13] [c15] [c16]
- **Chinese: tiger and centipede on the same garment.** Qing children's tiger bibs carried a centipede and the other poisons; the tiger scared demons off, and 獨 dú 'solitary' puns on 毒 dú 'poison', so the tiger sometimes joins the set. This is the one tradition where Scorpio's creature and the Tiger literally share a cloth. Strongest crossing-specific item. [c14] [c16]
- **Japan: Ōmukade 大百足 ōmukade.** In the Tawara Tōda legend, Fujiwara no Hidesato shoots a giant centipede living on Mount Mikami 三上山 that preyed on the dragon clan of Lake Biwa; two arrows bounce off, the third, wet with his saliva, kills it. [c17] [c18]
- **Japan: messenger of Bishamonten 毘沙門天 Bishamonten.** The centipede is held to be the messenger of the guardian deity Bishamonten. Merchants used it in crests and shop names: many legs for 'many customers' feet' and strength for 'never losing to other shops'; in war it was tied to archery and victory. [c18] [c19]
- **Japan: silk farmers of Miyagi.** At Fukuoji Bishamondo (Kakuda and Marumori area), farmers offered centipede ema plaques to protect silkworms, because the centipede was the rat's natural enemy; about 300 are displayed and over 23,000 stored, listed as Important Tangible Folk Cultural Properties. [c19]
- **Chinese proverb 百足之蟲，死而不僵 bǎi zú zhī chóng, sǐ ér bù jiāng.** Do not use for this animal: 百足之蟲 is glossed as millipede, not centipede. Borrowed-species risk. [c21]

## The fusion (our reading)
- **Temperament.** Our reading: fixed Water (Scorpio) feeds yang Wood (Tiger). Scorpio gathers and waits; the Tiger commits and takes ground. The Giant Centipede is the patient ambusher on the cave ceiling: anchored, unseen, then holding prey nearly double its own weight. The Duanwu tradition already put tiger and centipede on one child's bib as guards against harm, so this crossing reads as the protector who is feared.
- **Gifts.** Our reading: courage backed by preparation; the grip to hold what others cannot (anchored rear legs, seven or eight front pairs on the prey, c7); fierce protection of its own (the guardian on the bib, the silkworm guard); stamina in the dark hours.
- **Shadow.** Our reading: Scorpio control plus Tiger domineering; intensity that frightens people before they know you; the Five Poisons side: words that sting longer than intended; Tiger impetuousness breaking Scorpio's patience at the wrong moment, like the centipede that falls from the ceiling still gripping the bat (c7).
- **Love.** Our reading: slow to let anyone near, then a hold that does not loosen. Scorpio possessiveness meets the Tiger's need for adventure, so the partner must be both loyal and lively. Protective to the point of guarding.
- **Money.** Our reading: Japanese merchant lore made the centipede a sign of many customers and never losing to the shop next door (c18). Ambitious and competitive with money, plays to win, can overreach when the Tiger's confidence outruns Scorpio's research.
- **Conflict.** Our reading: does not posture. Stays still, chooses the ground, then strikes once and hard. Forgives slowly. Needs to watch the sting: the Five Poisons were feared for a reason.
