## The Swan as a symbol
- **Chinese, Shiji (Sima Qian), ch. 48 Chen She Shijia.** 燕雀安知鴻鵠之志哉 (yànquè ān zhī hónghú zhī zhì zāi), 'how could swallows and sparrows know the ambition of the great swan?' Chen She says it as a young hired plowman resting at the edge of a field, when his fellow laborers laugh at his talk of rising. The idiom means small minds cannot grasp a far-reaching aim. For an Ox crossing this is a gift: the swan's ambition is spoken by a man behind a plow. [s15] [s16]
- **Hindu (Sanskrit hamsa).** The hamsa is the mount of Saraswati (knowledge, the arts) and Brahma. It was said to separate milk from water, used as a metaphor for telling good from bad; Paramahamsa, 'supreme hamsa', is a title for realized sages. Caution: scholars dispute whether the hamsa is a swan or the bar-headed goose (Vogel 1952 vs Dave). Present it as 'the hamsa, often translated swan'. The discernment image sits naturally beside Libra's scales. [s12]
- **Greek.** The swan was sacred to Apollo and stood for harmony and beauty. The swan-song belief was proverbial by the 3rd century BCE; in Plato's Phaedo Socrates says swans sing most beautifully before death, for joy, not sorrow. Pliny the Elder called the story false. Note: the mute swan, the familiar European species, does not sing as it dies; whooper and tundra swans have the tracheal loop that may explain the legend. [s13]
- **Irish, Oidheadh Chlainne Lir (Children of Lir), one of the Three Sorrows of Storytelling.** Aoife, jealous stepmother, turns Lir's four children into swans for 900 years across three waters; they keep human speech and song. When they leave for the Sea of Moyle it is proclaimed that no swan should be killed in Ireland. A story of endurance and keeping one's voice through long hardship, which suits the Ox half. [s14]
- **Chinese and northern Eurasian swan-maiden tales.** The oldest recorded swan-maiden tale is Chinese, in Gan Bao's Sou shen ji (4th century); in Buryat ancestor myths a hunter marries a swan maiden and their children found clans such as the Khori. Use only as one sentence; the wider motif is generic. [s19]
- **British and Danish.** The British monarch holds rights over unmarked mute swans in open water, shared with the Vintners' and Dyers' Companies since the 15th century; the mute swan has been Denmark's national bird since 1984. Trivia, low citable value; do not lead with it. [s6]
- **Unsourced, do not use.** Generic 'swan spirit animal means grace, purity, rebirth' lines found on totem blogs; no traditional source located. 

## Header hanzi verdict
Live page shows 鵠 hú in rail__han and serial-han, with pinyin hú. Verdict: CORRECT but classical. MDBG gives 鵠 hú = swan (and gǔ = bull's-eye of a target) [s17]; it is the swan of 鴻鵠 hónghú in the Shiji idiom [s15]. Modern everyday word is 天鵝 tiān'é; the mute swan is 疣鼻天鵝 yóubí tiān'é, with the old name 丹鵠 dān hú [s18]. Keep 鵠 hú; optional caption 天鵝 tiān'é. Small consistency note: the header is traditional (鵠) while the proverb tiles are simplified (任劳任怨).

## The fusion (our reading)
- **Temperament.** Our reading: cardinal Air (Libra) on yin Earth (Ox). Libra sets the tone of the water and wants it level; the Ox does the slow work that keeps it so. The Swan is the composed worker: the plowman Chen She with a swan's aim, a creature that makes poise look free because nobody watches the labor.
- **Gifts.** Our reading: the hamsa's discernment, sorting the true part of an argument from the noise (Libra's scales with an Ox's patience); loyalty that holds through many seasons; calm that settles a group; force held in reserve and used only for what it has claimed.
- **Shadow.** Our reading: 任勞任怨 rèn láo rèn yuàn, the page's own proverb, read as warning: bearing labor and complaint so silently that others assume the load is light. Libra's wish to keep the surface pleasant and the Ox's stubbornness combine into a refusal to say 'this is heavy'. When it finally breaks, it breaks like the wing-arched charge: sudden and out of proportion.
- **Love.** Our reading: Venus-ruled and Ox-loyal. Slow to pair, then steady across years; shows love by carrying (the cygnets on the back) and guarding (the nesting cob). Needs a partner who asks twice and does not take 'fine' as an answer. Courtship matters: Libra likes the ritual, the Ox likes the proof.
- **Money.** Our reading: Ox thrift with Libra taste. Saves steadily, then spends on one beautiful, durable thing for the home. Risk: overpaying to keep the peace, or never asking for the raise because asking feels like disturbing the water.
- **Conflict.** Our reading: goes smooth, holds position, waits for the other side to tire. Rarely starts a fight; defends territory hard once it does. The hamsa's skill applies here too: separate the one point that matters and let the rest float off.

## Fact audit of the live page
- **WRONG (incubation clause)**: Mute swans typically form pair bonds that last for the bird's lifetime, with both parents sharing incubation and cygnet care.
  Lifelong pairing is broadly right: Minton (1968) found about 85% kept the same living mate year to year, divorce 3% in breeders and 9% in non-breeders [s8]; Avon WT repeats the 3% [s9]. But the female does the incubating; the male guards and covers the eggs while she feeds, and has no brood patch [s7]. Rewrite: 'both parents guard the nest and raise the cygnets; the female incubates'. [s7] [s8] [s9]
- **WRONG (continuous)**: A swan's smooth glide is powered by continuous paddling of its legs below the waterline, entirely hidden from anyone watching.
  Swans paddle to swim, but resting swans commonly float with one foot lifted out of the water onto the back, a normal behavior in all swan species, likely for warming or cooling [s7] [s10]. Wikipedia also notes mute swans can drift several hundred meters on the wind in the busking posture [s6]. The 'legs never stop' idea is the duck-paddling proverb, not biology. The hero line ('your legs never stop moving') rests on it too. Rewrite: 'swims with strokes of webbed feet hidden below the surface'. [s6] [s7] [s10]
- **OK**: When a nest or cygnets are threatened, a mute swan will arch its wings over its back and charge, a defense strong enough to injure an approaching predator or person.
  Busking display: neck back, wings half raised; defense escalates to hissing, wing-spur strikes and bites; swans attack canoes and dogs and can drown large dogs [s6]; males are the aggressive ones [s7]. A nesting swan's attack tipped a kayaker who drowned near Des Plaines, Illinois, in April 2012 (species not named in the AP report) [s11]. Suggest 'the male' rather than 'a mute swan' and 'half-raised' rather than 'over its back'. [s6] [s7] [s11]
- **OK (reason unverifiable)**: Cygnets sometimes ride on a parent's back, tucked near the nape, especially in cold water or when predators are near.
  Tired cygnets climb onto a parent's back and are carried [s6]; SWT says small cygnets are often carried on the mother's back [s7]. 'Especially in cold water or when predators are near' is not in either source; cut or soften to 'when tired'. [s6] [s7]
- **UNVERIFIABLE**: Long neck and level flight posture give the mute swan a near-silhouette of a single unbroken curve, among the most recognizable outlines in wetland birds.
  Subjective superlative with no source. Cut, or replace with a checkable fact (the S-curved neck used to reach submerged plants [s6]). [s6]
- **OK (with exception)**: Breeding pairs defend a stretch of water as territory, often the same site year after year, tolerating few other swans within it.
  Usually strongly territorial, one pair on smaller lakes, same nest restored yearly [s6] [s7]. Exception worth knowing: where food is rich they nest in colonies, over 100 pairs at Abbotsbury Swannery with nests as close as 2 m [s6]. [s6] [s7]
- **OK**: Header hanzi 鵠 (hú) means swan.
  鵠 hú = swan (MDBG); classical, the 鴻鵠 hónghú of the Shiji idiom. Modern word 天鵝 tiān'é; mute swan 疣鼻天鵝 yóubí tiān'é. [s15] [s17] [s18]
- **OK**: Proverb 任劳任怨 (rèn láo rèn yuàn), bear labor and bear complaint, tied to the Year of the Ox as our own pairing.
  Real idiom with correct gloss; the page honestly labels the pairing as its own. Not separately sourced here; meaning matches standard dictionaries. Consider the stronger, sourced Shiji line 燕雀安知鴻鵠之志 instead or as well, since it actually names the swan [s15]. [s15]
